Clustering method review

Skill taxonomy, Section B, Sprint 3. Based on the 11,449-skill run of 2026-10-22. observed = measured on our data. hypothesis = needs a test. recommend = what to do.

A. Executive recommendation

observedFour new checks on the existing runs change the picture.

0.78AMI between HDBSCAN (10, ms1) and Leiden (res 20), on the 78% of skills HDBSCAN clusters. The two methods mostly agree. The real choice is about the other 22%.
48%of Leiden res-20 communities have <90% of their members inside one res-5 community. Leiden resolutions are not nested.
29% vs 21%HDBSCAN noise rate for strings of ≤4 characters vs longer strings. Short strings (6.6% of skills) are harder.
22.5% vs 19.5%Noise rate for skills seen once vs skills seen 5+ times. Noise is not mainly a rare-skill problem.

Primary clustering method

recommendUse Leiden on a kNN graph as the topic level, run with CPM instead of modularity. Each skill also gets a confidence score, and low-confidence skills are marked "weak" instead of being trusted. Keep HDBSCAN as a density check.

hypothesisThis is a starting choice, not a verified winner. Leiden has fewer moving parts (no UMAP step, and one main setting). CPM keeps its granularity steady as the skill set grows each week (Traag et al. 2011). The final pick should go to whichever method wins the stability test and the human intrusion test (section E). If they tie, choose Leiden for the reasons above.

Noise and ambiguous points

recommendUse three states, never a silent forced assignment: assigned (high confidence), weak (has a best topic but low margin) and unassigned. Confidence combines the similarity to the topic centroid with the margin over the second-best topic. Search can use weak skills, but the report must count them separately.

Hierarchy

recommendRun average-linkage agglomerative clustering over topic centroids, not over raw skills. Cut the tree into about 20–40 families, then have a human review the families. Do not use Leiden at several resolutions as parent and child levels, because they do not nest (48% above).

Embeddings and acronyms

recommendFirst, merge verified aliases (excel = ms excel = microsoft excel). Then run one small probe experiment on 300 skills: raw string vs a context sentence vs a larger model. Adopt a change only if it raises neighbour precision@5 clearly (section B, Q4). Do not assume a bigger model fixes acronyms.

Incremental updates

recommendEvery day, assign new skills by a kNN vote with a confidence threshold. Every week, re-cluster in batch. Then map the new clusters onto persistent topic IDs with Jaccard overlap plus Hungarian matching, with explicit split and merge rules. Incremental community detection is not worth the extra work at our size.

Evaluation metrics

recommendReport five groups and never one number:

  • Coherence: mean intra-topic cosine and a human word-intrusion test.
  • Stability: AMI on 80% subsamples, plus per-topic Jaccard.
  • Coverage: the assigned share, counted per skill and per mention.
  • External: ESCO/O*NET alignment, used as a diagnostic only.
  • Downstream: a small initiative → skills retrieval check.

Drop silhouette as a headline number.

B. Critical assessment of our current results

  1. Silhouette is not comparable across our runs. For HDBSCAN it was computed only on clustered points, so the hard 22–36% was left out. That biases the score upward. It was also computed in 384-d space, while HDBSCAN clustered in UMAP space. Every other method was scored on 100% of points. A method can "win" silhouette just by refusing hard points.
  2. One seed, no stability. Every run used seed 42. UMAP is stochastic, and so are Leiden's tie-breaks. We do not yet know whether 388 or 323 topics would reappear with another seed or another sample.
  3. Methods were compared by eyeballing the head. We looked at the top clusters sorted by mentions, which are the easiest ones. Junk sits in the tail (for example the acronym community). A fair review needs a random sample of topics.
  4. Clustering is doing alias normalisation too. Many clusters are alias sets (excel / ms excel / microsoft excel). With lowercase+trim keys, the algorithm spends its capacity merging spellings. That inflates "coherence" and hides the real topic structure. Normalise first, then cluster canonical skills.
  5. The unit of analysis is unweighted strings. 77% of strings appear once, and some are extraction noise (F1 .66 strict). "communication" (1,000+ mentions) counts the same as "a1 finish". Report coverage both per skill and per mention (22% vs 17% noise already shows the gap).
  6. Ambiguity cannot be solved by context-free embeddings. observed "networking" lands with TCP/IP and DHCP in HDBSCAN, but with prospecting and customer service in Leiden res 1. Both readings are real. One vector per string cannot hold both.
  7. Modularity resolution depends on graph size. With RBConfiguration (modularity), the same resolution value gives different granularity as the inventory grows. That breaks weekly comparability. CPM does not have this problem (Traag et al. 2011).
  8. The noise diagnosis was wrong. observed Singletons are noise at 22.5% and frequent skills at 19.5%. So "cluster only skills with ≥2 mentions" will not cut noise much. The noise is generic or polysemous phrases (attention to detail, judgment), not rare ones.
  9. The sample is small compared with the corpus. We have 1,931 of about 124k postings. The topic set will grow and change, so stability under growth (a 50% → 100% subset) matters as much as stability under resampling.

Answers to questions 1–7

Q1. Which method should be primary? Also graph construction

observedHDBSCAN and Leiden res 20 agree strongly where HDBSCAN is confident (AMI 0.78). So "which is better" really means "what should happen to the 22% that are hard?"

  • HDBSCAN: honest about uncertainty, and good for spotting junk. But it depends on UMAP (stochastic, with extra settings), it collapses at min_cluster_size 30, and a noise label is not a usable state for search.
  • Leiden: full coverage, fast, one setting, and naturally graph-based (it fits kNN assignment of new skills). But it hides weak points inside communities.
  • Agglomerative on raw skills: very sensitive to the threshold (9 → 5,276 clusters), and average linkage chains on high-dimensional data. Do not use it as the flat method. It is good over centroids for the hierarchy.
  • Hybrid (recommended): Leiden partition + a per-skill confidence score + HDBSCAN noise as a second flag. The skills that both methods find uncertain form the review queue.

Evidence needed to pick Leiden over HDBSCAN:

  1. Subsample AMI at least as high.
  2. Human intrusion accuracy on random topics no lower than 5 points below HDBSCAN.
  3. Weak-flagged skills judged wrong more often than assigned skills. This shows the flag works.

Graph construction

  • Union vs mutual kNN: embedding spaces have hubs, points that are near everything (Radovanović et al. 2010). Generic skills like "communication" become hubs and glue topics together. A mutual kNN graph drops one-way edges into hubs, but it can isolate points. Test both.
  • k = 15 vs 30: a larger k connects more and gives smoother, larger communities, but it adds weak edges.
  • Thresholds: pick an edge cut-off from the empirical similarity distribution (for example the 10th percentile of kNN similarities). BGE cosine values are compressed toward the high end, so a fixed number like 0.5 means little.
  • Weights: cosine is fine to start. Shared-nearest-neighbour (Jaccard of neighbour lists) weighting dampens hubs and is common in single-cell graph clustering.

recommendControlled sweep: k ∈ {15, 30} × {union, mutual} × CPM resolution at 4 values chosen to give about 150–500 communities. That is 16 runs of a few seconds each. Score each with stability and coverage, then take the top 2 to human review.

Q2. Is silhouette meaningful?

Silhouette favours compact, well-separated, roughly convex groups (Rousseeuw 1987). Skill space is a continuum: "data analysis → financial analysis → variance analysis" has no gaps. Useful topics can therefore score near 0. Our values (0.02–0.14) say "no sharp gaps", not "bad topics". We also computed it on different subsets per method (see B1).

Comparing partitions correctly

  • Relabelling: AMI and ARI ignore label names, so no matching is needed (Vinh et al. 2010; Hubert & Arabie 1985). Prefer AMI when cluster counts differ.
  • Different subsets: compare two 80% subsamples on their intersection (about 64% of skills).
  • HDBSCAN noise: report it two ways. (a) AMI on points clustered in both runs. (b) Agreement on noise status itself. Do not treat −1 as one big cluster, because that inflates AMI.
Q3. How should the hierarchy be built?

observedLeiden partitions are not nested: 48% of res-20 communities split across res-5 parents at the 90% purity level. Forcing them into a tree would create false parent links.

recommendTake the topic centroids (about 300), run average-linkage cosine agglomerative clustering, cut into about 20–40 families, and have a human review them. It is explainable (a dendrogram), cheap, and re-runnable each week. The HDBSCAN condensed tree is valid but tied to HDBSCAN and hard to explain.

Representation: use SKOS-style relations (broader/narrower inside our taxonomy). Use exactMatch/closeMatch/broadMatch/narrowMatch to ESCO/O*NET in a separate mapping table (W3C SKOS). A skill with no external match simply has no mapping row and still lives in our tree. Multiple membership stays in skill_topic with a role (primary/secondary), so the tree itself stays a clean tree.

Q6. Stable IDs across re-clusters

There are three kinds of ID:

  • skill_id: permanent and never reused.
  • topic_id: persistent across runs.
  • cluster_id: run-specific (it is what the algorithm outputs).

recommendAfter each weekly run:

  1. Build W[old, new] = Jaccard of member skill_ids, using only skills present in both runs.
  2. Run Hungarian matching on −W (Kuhn 1955).
  3. Continuation: a matched pair with J ≥ 0.5 keeps its topic_id. hypothesis Tune the threshold on a dry run.
  4. Split: an old topic where ≥2 new clusters each have containment |o∩n|/|n| ≥ 0.7. The largest one keeps the ID, and the others get new IDs with a split_from link.
  5. Merge: the same rule in reverse, with merged_into links.
  6. New topic: no old topic with J ≥ 0.2. Retired: an old topic that matches nothing gets status=retired and is never deleted.
  7. Labels: keep the old label unless more than 50% of members changed, then queue it for human relabel. Use centroid cosine as a tie-breaker.

This follows the event model of Greene et al. (2010). Incremental Leiden is not worth it: a weekly batch over about 20–50k skills takes seconds to minutes.

Decision criteria (fixed before looking)

  1. Drop any config with subsample AMI below 0.6 or median topic Jaccard below 0.5. hypothesis These thresholds follow Hennig's guidance that per-cluster Jaccard under 0.5 means a dissolved cluster.
  2. Among the rest, pick the higher human intrusion accuracy. A difference under 5 points is a tie.
  3. Break ties by mention coverage of the assigned state, then by simplicity (Leiden).
  4. The weak flag is valid only if weak skills are judged wrong at least twice as often as assigned skills.

F. Python implementation guidance

Build the kNN graph (union or mutual) 
import numpy as np, igraph as ig
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

def knn_graph(X, k=15, mutual=False, min_sim=None):
    dist, nbr = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=k + 1, metric="cosine").fit(X).kneighbors(X)
    sim, nbr = 1 - dist[:, 1:], nbr[:, 1:]
    pairs = {}
    for i in range(len(X)):
        for s, j in zip(sim[i], nbr[i]):
            pairs.setdefault((min(i, j), max(i, j)), []).append(s)
    edges, w = [], []
    for (i, j), s in pairs.items():
        if mutual and len(s) < 2:          # one-way edge: drop in mutual mode
            continue
        if min_sim is not None and s[0] < min_sim:
            continue
        edges.append((i, j)); w.append(float(np.mean(s)))
    g = ig.Graph(n=len(X), edges=edges); g.es["weight"] = w
    return g

# per-topic Jaccard (Hennig 2007): for each reference topic, best Jaccard in each rerun
def best_jaccard(ref_members, other_labels, other_idx):
    sets = {}
    for i, l in zip(other_idx, other_labels):
        sets.setdefault(l, set()).add(i)
    return max(len(ref_members & s) / len(ref_members | s) for s in sets.values())

Match old and new topic IDs 
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
def match(old: dict, new: dict, keep=0.5):
    """old/new: {id: set(skill_ids)}; compare only skills present in both runs."""
    shared = set().union(*old.values()) & set().union(*new.values())
    O = {k: v & shared for k, v in old.items()}; N = {k: v & shared for k, v in new.items()}
    ok, nk = list(O), list(N)
    W = np.array([[len(O[a] & N[b]) / max(1, len(O[a] | N[b])) for b in nk] for a in ok])
    r, c = linear_sum_assignment(-W)
    cont = {nk[j]: ok[i] for i, j in zip(r, c) if W[i, j] >= keep}
    return cont, W   # use W containment rules for split / merge / new / retired
Assign a new skill with confidence 
def assign(v, X, labels, k=15, t_vote=0.6, t_sim=0.75):
    sims = X @ v; nn = np.argsort(-sims)[:k]
    votes = {}
    for j in nn:
        votes[labels[j]] = votes.get(labels[j], 0) + sims[j]
    best, score = max(votes.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1])
    share = score / sum(votes.values())
    if sims[nn[0]] < t_sim:      return None, "unassigned", share
    return best, ("assigned" if share >= t_vote else "weak"), share

G. Evaluation report template

  1. Data snapshot: postings, mentions, unique and canonical skills, alias merges, extraction F1, and the share of skills by mention count.
  2. Setup: embedding model + revision, graph settings, seeds, and the hardware.
  3. Table 1, sweep: config → number of topics, coverage (skill and mention), mean intra cosine, AMI mean±sd.

    • margin histogram, coloured by state
    • per-topic Jaccard stability vs size
    • dendrogram of families
    • a UMAP 2D map, for illustration only
  7. Examples: 5 strong topics, 5 weak topics (honestly chosen at random), and 3 split/merge events from the ID matching dry run.
